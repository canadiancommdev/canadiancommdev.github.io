Small Business Planning in Canada

How to Write a Small Business Plan in Canada

A practical starting point for Canadian entrepreneurs, small business owners and the organizations that support them.

Home  ›  Knowledge Centre  ›  Small Business Planning

A business plan is useful when it helps you make decisions. For a small business in Canada, that means setting out who you will serve, how the business will operate, what it will cost and how the numbers support the next step. It is not a promise that a lender or funder will say yes. It is a clear, evidence-based case for the business.

By Lucille Timmons, Community Economic Development Professional · LinkedIn profile

Start with the customer and the problem

1. Be specific about who will buy

Describe the customers you intend to serve and the need your product or service meets. “Everyone” is rarely a useful target market. Look for practical evidence: conversations with potential customers, local population and visitor information, competitor research, pre-orders, survey results or early sales.

Explain what customers use now, what alternatives they have and why they would choose your offer. If the business depends on seasonal visitors, repeat buyers or a small local market, say so and show how that affects sales.

Explain how the business works

2. Describe the offer and day-to-day operation

Set out what you sell, how customers find and purchase it, where the work happens and what is needed to deliver it. Include suppliers, equipment, staffing, permits or insurance where relevant. Identify the owner’s role and the skills or outside support the plan depends on.

A plan should also make operating limits visible. Capacity, opening hours, delivery area, seasonality and available owner time can all affect realistic sales and costs.

Show the financial logic

3. Build projections from clear assumptions

Estimate sales using a method you can explain: number of customers, average purchase, repeat rate, available appointment slots or expected occupancy. Then list the costs required to earn that revenue, including supplies, rent, utilities, insurance, wages, loan payments, marketing, taxes and owner compensation where applicable.

Prepare a monthly cash-flow forecast for the first year and annual projections for the following years. Cash flow and profit answer different questions. A business can show a profit on paper and still run short of cash if customer payments arrive after bills are due.

Useful check: Change the assumptions. What happens if sales take longer to build, costs rise or the busy season is shorter than expected? A sensible plan explains how the owner would respond.
Connect the request to the plan

4. Explain how much money is needed and why

If you are approaching a lender or seeking funding, specify the amount, the costs it will cover and the timing. Match each requested expense to a quote, estimate or other support where possible. Explain what the money is expected to help the business achieve, and how repayment or ongoing operating costs will be handled.

Programs, eligibility rules and deadlines change. Confirm current details directly with the relevant lender or funder before relying on them. A business plan can organize the case, but it cannot replace a program’s requirements.

Make it useful after submission

5. Finish with milestones and risks

Turn the plan into a short action schedule. Identify the next steps, who is responsible, what each step costs and what must happen first. Include meaningful risks—such as delayed approvals, supplier changes, slow sales or a key-person absence—and a realistic response for each.

Review the plan when important assumptions change. It should help guide decisions after a loan application or funding request is submitted, not sit untouched in a folder.

Common small business planning gaps

  • Using broad claims about demand without showing how they were checked
  • Projecting sales without showing the calculation behind them
  • Leaving out working capital, taxes, owner pay or maintenance costs
  • Confusing a profitable year with having enough cash each month
  • Requesting funding without connecting each cost to a clear business need
  • Using old program details without checking current eligibility and deadlines

Who can use a structured planning system?

Owners can use a step-by-step system to organize the business case, financial model and action plan. Community Futures teams, economic development officers, chambers, business associations and other business-support organizations can use a consistent framework to help clients prepare and discuss their plans.

The Small Business Success Planning System brings together a five-year business plan, financial projections, funding preparation and action planning. If your project also needs a broader funding and project structure, see the Government Funding & Project Planning System.

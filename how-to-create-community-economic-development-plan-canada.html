What Makes a Strong Community Economic Development Plan in Canada

A high-level look at why the major planning areas matter, with a few practical examples and the common mistakes that can weaken the process.

Home  ›  Knowledge Centre  ›  Community Economic Development Planning

A community economic development plan should help a community make better choices, focus limited resources and move realistic priorities toward action. The value is not in producing a long document. It is in creating a shared direction that people can actually use.

Broad phase one

Understanding the community

This phase matters because communities can waste time and funding when decisions are based on assumptions rather than current local conditions. A strong plan begins with a clear understanding of what is changing, what is working and where the most important gaps or opportunities exist.

Example: A community may believe it needs more tourism attractions when the larger issue is that existing experiences are difficult to find, book or connect into a full visitor itinerary.
Broad phase two

Recognizing local strengths and assets

This phase matters because community development is stronger when it builds from existing people, knowledge, businesses, infrastructure and relationships. Focusing only on problems can hide practical opportunities that are already within reach.

Example: An underused community building, a strong volunteer network and several home-based businesses may create a realistic starting point for a local market or business-support initiative.
Broad phase three

Choosing priorities

This phase matters because no organization or community can pursue every idea at once. Priorities help leaders decide what deserves attention now, what requires more preparation and what should wait.

Example: Instead of listing ten unrelated projects, a community may decide that improving small-business readiness is the priority that will support several other goals over time.
Broad phase four

Preparing projects for funding and action

This phase matters because a good idea is not automatically a fundable or deliverable project. Funders and partners need to see that the need, expected results, responsibilities, resources and long-term implications have been considered.

Example: A proposal for a new community facility is much stronger when the organization can clearly show who will use it, who will operate it and how ongoing costs will be managed.
Broad phase five

Turning the plan into implementation

This phase matters because many plans lose momentum after approval. A useful plan creates accountability, keeps priorities visible and gives the organization a way to review progress and adapt when conditions change.

Example: A community may begin with one achievable partnership project while larger priorities continue through funding development and approval.

Common mistakes that weaken a community economic development plan

  • Starting with a preferred project before confirming the real need
  • Trying to include too many priorities for the available capacity
  • Relying on the same small group of participants for every decision
  • Choosing projects mainly because a funding program exists
  • Approving a plan without clear responsibility for moving it forward
  • Treating the completed document as the end of the process

Why organizations use a complete planning system

Understanding the broad phases is only the beginning. The time-consuming work is creating the assessments, worksheets, decision tools, project structure, funding preparation, implementation records and reporting process that connect the phases into one usable system.

The Community Economic Development Planning System provides that structure in a professional, fillable format so organizations do not have to design the entire planning process from scratch.
